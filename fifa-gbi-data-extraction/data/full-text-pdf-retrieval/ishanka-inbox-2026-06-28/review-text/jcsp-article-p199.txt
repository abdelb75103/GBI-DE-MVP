     Although referees who ofﬁciate in the amateur football leagues are exposed to
     various stressors that can negatively affect their mental health (MH), little is
     known about their MH symptoms. The purpose of the study was to evaluate MH
     symptoms of referees who ofﬁciate in the Turkish amateur football leagues. An
     online survey was sent to all referees in the Turkish amateur football leagues
     (n = 4,900) incorporating standardized scales assessing depression, anxiety, and
     stress. A total of 1,279 referees participated in the study. Female referees reported
     higher depression (p < .01) and anxiety (p = .02) scores than males. Younger
     referees (23–27 years) reported higher depression (p = .01) and anxiety (p < .01)
     scores than older (>38 years) referees. Results showed that symptoms of
     depression, anxiety, and stress scores were associated with marital status (being
     single), lower incomes, severe sports injury history, and inadequate social
     support. In light of these results, MH assessments should be undertaken to detect
     which referees are at greater risk of MH problems and facilitate appropriate and
     timely MH interventions. Further study is needed to inform MH risk reduction
     strategies and/or programming.

     Keywords: sports ofﬁcials, depression, anxiety, stress, soccer

physical, professional, and psychological demands (Arnold & Fletcher, 2012).
While referees run almost as much as the players throughout a match (Weston
et al., 2012), they also manage the game (Guillén & Feltz, 2011) and deal with
unique stressors such as abuse (e.g., microaggressions such as bad language or

physical assault, verbal, or physical threats) from players, coaches, or spectators on
ﬁeld, in the corridor, or locker rooms (Mountjoy et al., 2016; Webb, Rayner, et al.,
2020). While meeting the responsibilities of their role, referees must make accurate
decisions despite potential economic consequences for athletes, teams, and clubs
(Webb, 2017). Referees work in stressful environments and have developed
various coping strategies to deal with these issues (Cuskelly & Hoye, 2013).
Older age, greater refereeing experience, and adequate social and organizational
support all provide advantages when coping with these issues (Cuskelly & Hoye,
2013; Hong et al., 2019). Those who cannot create adequate coping strategies may
be negatively affected psychologically and be more vulnerable to mental health
(MH) problems such as depression, anxiety, and stress. Carson et al. (2020) and
Lima et al. (2022) report that negative emotional symptoms among referees are
associated with younger age, being single, reduced ofﬁciating experience, and
performance concerns. Likewise, research shows that referees who are less
satisﬁed with social support and have a severe injury history are more likely to
report MH problems than their counterparts (Gouttebarge et al., 2017; Kilic et al.,
2018). In addition, female referees are at greater risk of MH problems because
toxic, abusive, male-dominated environments that include sexist and derogatory
language negatively affect their MH (Webb et al., 2021).
     In amateur football refereeing, it is noticeable that referees are exposed to
diverse issues compared with referees at higher levels, which can negatively
impact their MH. For example, although verbal abuse is a signiﬁcant stressor that
threatens the MH of referees at all levels (Webb, Dicks, et al., 2020), amateur
league referees suffer from this abuse more frequently given higher rates of
negative behavior toward referees in amateur leagues (Webb, Rayner, et al.,
2020). Moreover, increased fear of physical harm due to the crowd’s proximity
to the ﬁeld, lack of physical barriers, and security staff at amateur levels are also
signiﬁcant sources of stress that may adversely affect the MH of referees who
ofﬁciate in these amateur leagues (Cuskelly & Hoye, 2013). Considering that these
referees are often younger, less experienced, and have lower levels of education
than referees who ofﬁciate in the professional leagues, they may also have
difﬁculty coping with the stressors mentioned above (Guillén & Feltz, 2011).
Conversely, amateur referees are not provided the technological tools used by
professional referees such as audio communication systems, goal-line technology,
and access to video assistant referees (e Pina et al., 2018). Furthermore, recent
research demonstrates that referees in lower leagues are more likely to suffer from
MH problems than their counterparts in higher leagues (Lima et al., 2022c).
     The COVID-19 pandemic created new stressors that may adversely affect the
MH of referees (Webb, 2021). Since the 2020–2021 amateur football season was
canceled in many countries, the income of referees who ofﬁciate in these amateur
leagues decreased. However, referees who had a second job during the pandemic
might have been protected from some of the more harmful ﬁnancial consequences
during that period. Given that the vast majority of amateur referees already had
second jobs, prior to the start of the pandemic, they may have prioritized their other
employment due to economic concerns (Wilson et al., 2020). Moreover, these
economic conditions may have been even more concerning for student referees
who tend to be younger, often do not have second jobs, and have additional
academic pressures (Sallen et al., 2018). When all of these factors are combined

      Although referees who ofﬁciate in the amateur football leagues are exposed to
unique stressors that can negatively affect their MH, there is a paucity of research
examining the MH problems of referees who ofﬁciate in lower leagues (Orviz-
Martínez et al., 2021). To ensure the timely implementation of protective measures,
it is necessary to understand the MH problems of referees and determine which
referees are at increased risk of MH problems. Therefore, the primary objective
of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of MH problems (anxiety, depression,
and stress) among referees who ofﬁciate in the Turkish amateur football leagues.
The secondary objective was to evaluate the association between MH problems
and a range of variables, including demographics, sport-speciﬁc aspects, and the
COVID-19 pandemic-related factors. Given results of previous research, it was
hypothesized that gender (being female), younger age, less refereeing experience,
lower income, being a student referee, and being infected with SARS-CoV-2
would be associated with MH problems.

                                        Method
Study Design and Data Collection
This cross-sectional, anonymous, online study was conducted in accordance
with the Helsinki declaration. The study was approved by the University Ethics
Committee (2021/1889-742844), and all participants provided informed consent.
The survey, questioning demographic information, sport-speciﬁc aspects, COVID-
19 pandemic-related factors, and three standardized scales, was sent to the
smartphones of referees who ofﬁciate in the Turkish amateur football leagues
via the Turkish Football Federation Medical Board. Data were collected between
October 2, 2021 and October 15, 2021, and conﬁdentiality of all data was ensured.

Participants
Turkish referees who ofﬁciate in the amateur football leagues were included in
this study. To be included in the study, referees had to be 18 years or older and
refereeing in the amateur football leagues throughout the 2021–2022 season. We
invited all referees (n = 4,900) registered in the Turkish Football Federation Referee
Board for the 2021–2022 season amateur leagues via online chat groups. A total of
1,279 referees completed the survey, with a response rate of 26.1%. No referees
were excluded from the study because all referees met the inclusion criteria.

Measures
Patient Health Questionnaire-9
The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) consisted of nine items to measure
depression according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-
IV (DSM-IV) criteria (Kroenke et al., 2001). Each item was scored on a 4-point
Likert-type scale: 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). Higher scores indicated

higher depression levels. Cutoff values of the PHQ-9 were reported for mild ≥5,
moderate ≥10, moderately severe ≥15, and severe ≥20 (Kroenke et al., 2001). The
PHQ-9 previously demonstrated sufﬁcient psychometrics and ﬁt indexes in a
Turkish sample (Güleç et al., 2012). Cronbach alpha internal reliability coefﬁcient
was .84, and the reliability coefﬁcient for this study was 0.85.

General Anxiety Disorder-7
The General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) consisted of seven items to measure
generalized anxiety disorder according to the DSM-IV criteria (Spitzer et al., 2006).
Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert-type scale: 0 (none) to 3 (almost every day).
Higher scores indicated a higher anxiety level. Cutoff values of the GAD-7 were
reported for mild ≥5, moderate ≥10, and severe ≥15 (Spitzer et al., 2006). The GAD-
7 previously demonstrated high validity, reliability, and good psychometric proper-
ties in a Turkish sample (Konkan et al., 2013). Cronbach alpha internal reliability
coefﬁcient was .85, and the reliability coefﬁcient for this study was 0.87.

Perceived Stress Scale-10
The Perceived Stress Scale-10 (PSS-10) consisted of 10 items to measure perceived
stress (Cohen et al., 1983). Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert-type scale:
0 (never) to 4 (very often). Higher scores indicated a higher stress level. Cutoff values
of the PSS-10 were reported for moderate ≥13 and severe ≥26 (Das.nh.gov., 2022).
The PSS-10 previously demonstrated good psychometric properties in a Turkish
sample (Eskin et al., 2013). Cronbach alpha internal reliability coefﬁcient was .82,
and the reliability coefﬁcient for this study was 0.78.

Demographic and COVID-19 Pandemic-Related Questions
Demographics included gender, age, marital status, highest education level, second
job, years in refereeing, annual income, severe sports injury history (leading to time
loss of more than 28 days in the last year), and psychiatric illness. The following
variables were collected to evaluate the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic: being
infected with SARS-CoV-2, adequate social support (thoughts about the provision
of adequate support from family and close friends), and performance concerns
(e.g., feeling useless as a referee and feeling a need to train harder for future
matches/contests).


Data Analysis
The data were analyzed using SPSS (version 24). Descriptive statistical methods
were utilized, with analysis incorporating frequency (n) and percentage (%). The
normality distribution was tested with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. As the data
did not show normal distribution (it exceeded the reference skewness–kurtosis
values of −1.95/+1.95), Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group compar-
isons, and Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for comparisons of three or more
groups. Spearman correlation test was applied to determine the direction and level
of correlation between scales. The level of signiﬁcance in the study was established
as .05 and .01.

female referees reported higher depression (p < .01) and anxiety (p = .02) scores

                                   Discussion
This is the ﬁrst study presenting the MH problems of referees who ofﬁciate in
amateur football leagues. The principal ﬁndings of the study were that 36.1% of
referees suffered from depression, 26.2% from anxiety, and 45.2% from stress;
the MH problems of referees were associated with being female, younger, single,
a student referee, having inadequate social support, and performance concerns.
     We hypothesized that the main reasons why almost one in two referees had
any MH problems were the pandemic, economic concerns, and abuse. The
pandemic and changes in football negatively affected referees as well as all
other football stakeholders (Webb, 2021). Moreover, the income of referees who
ofﬁciate in the amateur leagues, who were paid per match, decreased signiﬁcantly
due to the cancellation of the 2020–2021 amateur season. In accordance with our
hypothesis, while referees with another job may have focused on their second job
to make money, student referees and referees without another job may have faced
economic difﬁculties, leading to negative impacts on their MH (Wilson et al.,
2020). In addition, academic pressure may have made student referees even more
vulnerable to MH problems (Sallen et al., 2018). Furthermore, physical (bullying,
scufﬂing, etc.) or verbal (swearing, harassment, etc.) abuse from athletes, coaches,
or spectators is one of the primary stressors for referees (Mountjoy et al., 2016;
Webb, Dicks, et al., 2020), with referees who ofﬁciate in the amateur leagues
suffering this abuse more frequently (Webb, Rayner, et al., 2020). Nevertheless,
the high prevalence of MH problems among referees who ofﬁciate in the amateur
leagues is a worrying consequence. Therefore, underlying factors associated with
the poor MH outcomes of referees should be identiﬁed, while appropriate and
timely interventions should be provided.
     Although the use of different scales makes comparisons difﬁcult, our results
showed that the prevalence of MH symptoms of amateur referees was higher
compared with professional referees, elite athletes, and football players (Denerel &
Lima, 2022; Lima et al., 2021; Lima et al., 2022c). While Lima et al. reported that
34.6% of the professional league referees suffer from depression, 24% from

anxiety, and 40.6% from stress, these measures are 15%, 8.6%, and 22.4% for elite
athletes and 26.5%, 16.4%, and 19.3% for football players, respectively (Denerel
& Lima, 2022; Lima et al., 2021; Lima et al., 2022b; Nixdorf et al., 2013; Rice
et al., 2019). Although different stakeholders are exposed to both similar and
diverse stressors in sport, the fact that the prevalence of MH symptoms in amateur
referees is similar or higher than other groups demonstrates that further detailed
research is required.
     Recent adverse life events may affect the MH of athletes and may cause various
MH problems (Rice et al., 2016), and female athletes are more likely to experience
MH problems than males (Schaal et al., 2011). In a recent study conducted on the
same stakeholders, Lima et al. (2022a) reported that female football players suffered
from depression and stress almost ﬁve times more often than their male counterparts.
Otherwise, consistent with our results, Webb et al. reported that toxic, abusive, male-
dominated environments negatively affect the MH of female referees, and female
match ofﬁcials often experience poor MH during and after matches (Webb et al.,
2021). Although the number of female referees in our study is small, further
consideration of why female referees are more likely to suffer from depression and
anxiety is required. The risk factors that threaten their MH should be identiﬁed, and
timely interventions should be delivered.
     One of the most important elements that helps to deal with any stressors is social
support (Guillén & Feltz, 2011), with close family the most inﬂuential supporter
(Lin et al., 2017). Effective support assists referees in dealing with MH issues by
developing various coping strategies (Hong et al., 2019). In a recent study conducted
with Turkish professional football league referees, Lima et al. (2022c) revealed the
importance of social support on MH. Our results also support previous studies
showing that social and family support is essential for referees’ MH (Carson et al.,
2020; Kilic et al., 2018). Given that inadequate support may even cause discontinu-
ation from employment (Riggle et al., 2009), closely monitoring the referees who
thought that they had received insufﬁcient support and taking the necessary inter-
ventions will not only improve the MH status of amateur referees, but may also
remove an important barrier to being an excellent referee (Aragão e Pina et al., 2019).
     Older age and more refereeing experience contribute to the maintenance of
MH by providing autonomous behaviors and adaptive coping strategies (Cuskelly
& Hoye, 2013; Guillén & Feltz, 2011; Hong et al., 2019). Older referees can more
easily deal with stressors, which younger and less experienced referees face for the
ﬁrst time, by drawing upon their past experiences. Also, mental toughness and self-
efﬁciency are known to improve with age (Guillén & Feltz, 2011) and provide
some form of protection against MH problems (Slack et al., 2013). Moreover, a
recent study showed that younger and less experienced referees had an increased
risk of MH problems compared with their older and more experienced counterparts
(Lima et al., 2022c). Considering that younger referees in the present study are
usually students, whereas the vast majority of older referees have another job, the
distribution of having a second job according to age may also have impacted on
MH due to some of the possible reasons mentioned above (Wilson et al., 2020).
Since it is known that having more knowledge about stress makes it easier to
cope with stress, younger and less experienced referees, in particular, should be
informed about how to deal with stress through mental skills training (Mathers &
Brodie, 2011) and MH awareness programs (Breslin et al., 2017).

     Recent research on the same stakeholders has shown that being infected with
SARS-CoV-2 adversely affects athletes’ MH (Lima et al., 2021; Lima et al.,
2022b). Moreover, the MH problems of professional football players infected with
SARS-Cov-2 were associated with participation in football at lower levels and
performance concerns (Lima et al., 2022a). However, our result, which does not
support our hypothesis, can be explained by several reasons. Since it is known that
higher levels of education may afford greater levels of self-awareness and self-
regulation (Loghmani et al., 2021), referees could have developed coping strate-
gies more easily; while more than 80% of the football players in the study
mentioned above were high school graduates (Lima et al., 2021), this rate was
only 22.5% in the present study. Furthermore, the fact that the study was conducted
close to the third year of the pandemic may have caused us to achieve different
results compared with studies conducted in the early period of the pandemic. The
reduction of the restrictions, adaptation of the community to living with the
pandemic, having a greater knowledge of the SARS-CoV-2 infection, and reduc-
tion in mortality and morbidity due to SARS-CoV-2 may have tapered the negative
effects of being infected on MH (Our World in Data, 2022; Wessels et al., 2022).
     The capacity of referees for endurance and sprinting during matches is
essential to enable them to be in optimum positions on the ﬁeld of play, in order
to obtain sufﬁcient information to make accurate decisions (Giske et al., 2016).
This whole process is followed and reported by the referee observers (assessors)
on a match-by-match basis, and these reports and observations can affect the future
promotion opportunities of referees (Samuel, 2015). This unique stressor may put
referees under pressure and contribute to performance concerns. Performance
concerns not only negatively affect MH directly (Gouttebarge et al., 2017; Lima
et al., 2021) but may also cause sports injuries by leading to overloading, and
negatively affecting MH indirectly (Kilic et al., 2018). Given that referees with
performance concerns and severe injury history were at increased risk of MH
problems in the present study, it would be beneﬁcial to monitor the training
workload and the psychological states of referees with performance concerns.
Also, the MH of referees who have suffered from severe injuries should be
monitored as well as their physical health and adequate support should be provided
when they get injured and are recovering from injury.
     Several limitations should be considered for this study. First, there was no
clinical assessment for MH symptoms, and all outcome data were collected via
online self-reporting. Second, since the data were cross-sectional, results only
provide information about relationships and causality cannot be inferred, limiting
interpretations. Third, although a large sample size was achieved, the number of
female referees was far fewer than male referees, and therefore inadequate for
deﬁnitive determinations of gender effects. Fourth, the present study was limited to
referees who ofﬁciate in the Turkish amateur football leagues. Since the coping
strategies of referees living in various cultures and societies may differ (Arnold &
Fletcher, 2012), it may not be possible to generalize our results to all referees. Fifth,
questioning diagnosed psychiatric illness instead of subclinical conditions is also a
limitation. Sixth, the format of the responses to some questions as yes or no, closed
questions, limited the interpretation of the results. Lastly, some referees may have
worried about being stigmatized or identiﬁed, despite anonymity reassurance, and
therefore may not have completed the surveys accurately.

Practical Implications
The present study provides practical insights beyond the theoretical implications
that have been presented. Findings have particular relevance to referee adminis-
trators, trainers, and managers at governing bodies both in Turkey and in other
countries. Moreover, given the present concerns around the discontinuation and
attrition of football referees and sports ofﬁcials more widely (Dawson et al., 2022),
the present ﬁndings provide essential information for governing bodies that can be
used to improve referee mental health and the structures around referees, particu-
larly at a national level.
      First, the results identify amateur referees have an increased prevalence of
MH when compared with professional referees, elite athletes, and football players.
This presents challenges for administrators, with the recruitment and retention of
referees ever more important, given their discontinuation. As such, governing
bodies should focus on the support networks provided for newly qualiﬁed and
inexperienced referees to minimize drop out and improve MH.
      Second, the present study identiﬁed that female ofﬁcials were more likely to
suffer from depression and anxiety. Given the national and international drive to
recruit female referees and provide a more inclusive and supportive environment
for these referees when they do qualify, governing bodies and referee adminis-
trators should reﬂect on the current training, development and support provided
for female referees and whether this support provides the most effective framework
for good MH.
      Third, support for all referees should be as effective as possible. The present
study identiﬁed that effective social and family support is essential for positive
MH. Alongside a focus on this provision, governing bodies should also identify
the use of mentoring as a proactive method of supporting young and new referees.
By utilizing older, more experienced referees who are better prepared to deal with
stressors by reﬂecting on previous experiences, younger and less experienced
referees can utilize this experience through effective mentoring programs.
      Fourth, the organization of assessment for referees should be further consid-
ered by governing bodies. The ﬁndings identiﬁed that referees can feel pressurized,
and stressed by assessments and that performance concerns and severe injury
history provided an increased risk of MH problems. As such, monitoring training
workload and the psychological states of referees would be beneﬁcial for govern-
ing bodies. The approach to assessment and training preparation requires further
consideration, given the MH implications and the potential impact on referee
discontinuation and attrition rates.

                                    Conclusion
The present study contributes to an improved understanding of an under-re-
searched population and adds a valuable contribution to the extant literature by
showing the prevalence of depression, anxiety, stress symptoms, and correlating
variables among referees who ofﬁciate in the Turkish amateur football leagues.
Findings demonstrate that 36.1% of referees suffered from depression, 26.2%
from anxiety, and 45.2% from stress. Female, younger (23–27 years), and student
referees are especially prone to exhibit depression and anxiety symptoms. Our
